[5.1] ANALYSIS SCOPE & RESEARCH CONNECTION

Core question: How is social status associated with online food-ordering outcomes? SQ1 compares marital-status groups; SQ2 examines demographic and socioeconomic relationships; SQ3 compares customer-experience feedback across social groups.

This report uses the same 285 retained records as Assignment 4. The original CSV has 388 records; 103 full-row matches were excluded after normalization, and the unnamed column repeating Output was removed. No additional rows are deleted for this EDA.

The exclusion of matching rows is a documented assumption: without person identifiers, some matches could be different people. Output is a Yes/No outcome, not an order count or frequency. There are no dates, so calendar-time trends cannot be examined. Age-band patterns are explicitly descriptive.

[5.3] TEN EXPLORATORY VISUALIZATIONS

Each chart is followed by observation, interpretation, and research implication. These are exploratory findings; no causal conclusion or adjusted effect is claimed.

V01 · Age distribution

EDA

Observation: Age 23 is the most common age (47/285 records). 257/285 records (90.2%) are younger than 30.

Interpretation: The sample is concentrated among younger adults; its mean and median do not describe an even spread across ages.

Research implication: Discuss the concentration of younger respondents when applying the social-status findings to other populations.

Method: One-year bins centered on integer ages. Counts, not probability densities. No claim of statistically verified bimodality.

V02 · Age spread within marital-status groups

EDA

Observation: Median ages are Single 23.0, Married 27.0, and Prefer not to say 26.0; the last group has only 9 records.

Interpretation: Marital status and age vary together, so a marital-status comparison can also reflect differences in age composition.

Research implication: Treat age as a potential confounding variable when investigating whether marital status is associated with ordering.

Method: Boxes show Q1–Q3, the median, and whiskers to observed values within group-specific 1.5×IQR fences. Red points are flags, not deleted records.

V03 · Ordering outcomes by marital status

EDA

Observation: Single: 160/189 Yes (84.7%); Married: 53/87 (60.9%). The gap is 23.7 percentage points.

Interpretation: An observed ordering-outcome difference exists in this sample, but age, occupation, and other differences may contribute.

Research implication: This is the main descriptive evidence for SQ1. Test or model the association while accounting for other variables before making stronger claims.

Method: Percentages within marital-status groups. Output is binary; it does not measure order frequency.

V04 · Ordering outcomes by occupation

EDA

Observation: Student Yes rate is 87.5% (n=144); Housewife has only 9 records.

Interpretation: Occupation groups differ in both ordering outcomes and representation. Small-group percentages are less stable.

Research implication: Use this pattern to motivate SQ2 and explain why employment status should be evaluated alongside age and income.

Method: All four occupation groups are included, including the small Housewife group. This differs from A04 Q07, which used HAVING COUNT(*) >= 10.

V05 · Ordering outcomes by monthly income bracket

EDA

Observation: No Income: 85.5% Yes (n=131); More than 50000: 63.8% (n=47).

Interpretation: The income brackets show a descriptive difference; No Income may include students and does not identify a homogeneous financial group.

Research implication: Discuss income jointly with occupation and age under SQ2. Avoid treating a bracket as an exact monthly salary.

Method: Labels are source INR categories in logical display order. No numerical salaries were invented; no straight-line income correlation is calculated.

V06 · Age, household size, and ordering outcome

EDA

Observation: The unjittered Pearson correlation between age and family size is r=0.213. Both ordering outcomes occur across the plotted age and household-size combinations.

Interpretation: The scatter reveals overlap rather than an obvious separation of Yes and No. Any linear age–household relationship is described by the reported correlation.

Research implication: Consider multivariable relationships for SQ2 rather than using a single age or household-size threshold as a classifier.

Method: Seed-42 display jitter reduces overplotting; statistics use original integer values. Point color identifies the binary ordering outcome.

V07 · Correlation matrix: numeric and binary indicators

EDA

Observation: Age–Output correlation is -0.225; Family size–Output is -0.040; Output–Feedback is 0.549.

Interpretation: Pearson correlations with one binary indicator equal point-biserial correlations; with two binary indicators they equal phi. They describe pairwise association, not adjusted effects.

Research implication: Use age and household size as candidate predictors for SQ2. Keep feedback descriptive because its timing relative to ordering is unknown.

Method: Only numeric fields and explicitly 0/1 indicators are included. Marital status, occupation, and income are not assigned arbitrary numeric codes. Diagonal entries are self-correlations.

V08 · Ordering pattern across age bands

EDA

Observation: The 18–22 band has a 88.6% Yes rate (n=70); the 28–33 band has 61.7% (n=47).

Interpretation: The line connects ordered age categories for descriptive comparison. Smaller older-age groups can produce less stable rates.

Research implication: Use the age pattern to investigate whether marital-status differences partly reflect respondent age. This dataset cannot support changes in ordering over calendar time.

Method: Inclusive age bands 18–22, 23–27, and 28–33 cover all observed ages. The last band pools the sparse older ages rather than plotting the single age-33 record as its own group. Age is not time; no timestamps are available.

V09 · Feedback distribution by marital status

EDA

Observation: Positive feedback is 86.2% for Single, 72.4% for Married, and 55.6% for Prefer not to say.

Interpretation: Feedback distributions differ across social groups, but the small Prefer not to say group should not drive a broad conclusion.

Research implication: This addresses SQ3 with a customer-experience measure. Feedback is distinct from ordering outcome and should not be assumed available before an order.

Method: 100% stacked bars from Positive/Negative labels, including all retained records rather than only Output = Yes.

V10 · Missing-data patterns before and after cleaning

EDA

Observation: All 13 substantive fields have 0 missing cells in both the source CSV and the retained dataset.

Interpretation: There is no observed missingness pattern to impute in these fields. A complete table does not establish that the sample is representative or that every recorded value is correct.

Research implication: Missing data do not reduce this EDA sample; focus quality discussion on repeated records, small groups, and unusual but plausible values.

Method: Missingness checks empty values and common missing tokens. No Income and Prefer not to say are recorded categories, not converted to missing values. The redundant unnamed column is documented separately.

[5.5] FIVE FINDINGS TO DEVELOP IN THE RESULTS SECTION

  1. Marital-status difference: Single records have a 84.7% Yes rate versus 60.9% for Married, a 23.7-point gap. This supports investigating SQ1, with age composition considered as a possible confounder. [Evidence: V02–V03]
  2. Demographic and economic composition: The modal age is 23 (n=47); the No Income group has a 85.5% Yes rate. Student representation and related age/income patterns should be considered together under SQ2. [Evidence: V01, V04–V05]
  3. Pairwise associations: Age–Output r=-0.225; Family size–Output r=-0.040. These pairwise associations motivate multivariable analysis and do not establish causation. [Evidence: V06–V08]
  4. Feedback and social groups: Positive feedback is 86.2% for Single and 72.4% for Married. This offers descriptive evidence for SQ3; feedback remains distinct from the ordering outcome. [Evidence: V09]
  5. Data quality and scope: No substantive missing values were observed; 21 ages were IQR-flagged and retained. Matching records, small groups, and absent timestamps limit generalization and temporal claims. [Evidence: V10 and 5.4]

These are candidates for later Results writing. Report them with denominators and methodological limits, and distinguish exploratory patterns from confirmatory statistical evidence.
